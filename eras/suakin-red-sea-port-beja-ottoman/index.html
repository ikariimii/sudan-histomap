Suakin – Red Sea port (Beja, Ottoman)

Capitals / sites

Suakin

Geopolitics

Arab merchants settled at Sawakin in the 8th century (GHA III ch.3, p.82). The Mamluks took it a few years before 1272 (GHA IV ch.16, p.402); in 1264–5 Baybars punished its ruler, later made him the Mamluk representative, and set an annual tribute; the Mamluks retook it in the mid-15th century (p.418; see also ch.15, p.381). Özdemir put Sawakin under Ottoman suzerainty, and with Massawa (1557) it formed the province of Habesh; in 1571 the Funj, or the Beja, besieged it for three months (GHA V ch.6, p.175). Under the Mahdiyya, Osman Digna held the east except Sawakin and the Berber–Sawakin road (GHA VII ch.4, p.76).

Culture

Sawakin grew as a Muslim merchant town where Arabs ran the trade and Beja chiefs took shares (GHA IV ch.16, p.417). By the Funj period, the Hadariba led its trade, and the Ottomans chose the local amir from among them (GHA V ch.7, pp.177, 184).

Economy

From the Fatimids until the Portuguese, the Red Sea was the main route to India; Muslim merchants developed 'Aydhab and Sawakin, and Sawakin was probably Nubia's main outlet (GHA IV ch.16, pp.417–418). Baybars's tribute was 80 slaves, 300 camels and 30 kintars of ivory a year (p.418). Under the Funj, Sawakin was Sennar's main outlet by the 'Sudan Road' (GHA V ch.6, p.175; ch.7, p.181). The Hadariba traded slaves from Shendi for Indian goods, making Sawakin the main slave centre after Cairo and Massawa (ch.7, p.184). In the 19th century a railway from Suakin to Khartoum was planned (GHA VI ch.13, p.334).
