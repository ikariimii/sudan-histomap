Geopolitics

'Abd al-Karim b. Jame, who had studied in Bornu, founded Wadai in the 17th century (GHA V ch.17, p.511). Small kingdoms between Wadai and Darfur split their loyalty between the two sultanates (ch.7, p.187). Wadai paid tribute to Darfur as it had to the Tunjur, until Sultan Ya'qub stopped paying and invaded as far as Kabkabiyya; he was driven out (p.188). In the mid-18th century, Muhammad Jawda of Wadai defeated a Fur invasion and captured the Fur sultan (p.188). Tayrab of Darfur then made a treaty with Wadai that held for a hundred years (p.189). Wadai's isolation let it survive until 1911 (GHA VI ch.17, p.476).

Culture

Wadai fell within Bornu's political, cultural and commercial orbit (GHA V ch.17, pp.511–512). In the 19th century, the Sanusiyya brotherhood spread south along the Benghazi–Wadai route and mediated among the desert nomads (GHA VI ch.20, p.530; ch.17, p.473).

Economy

Wadai's sultans promoted a direct Benghazi–Wadai route that bypassed Bornu and Darfur; by 1900 it was more vital than any other trans-Saharan route (GHA VI ch.20, p.530). It sent slaves to Egypt into the late 19th century and carried the most modern repeating rifles into the desert (p.533). The rise of this route caused Darfur's Darb al-Arba'in to decline after the mid-century (p.530). Earlier, slaves from Bornu–Wadai were already sold in the Nile valley (GHA V ch.7, p.184).
