Geopolitics

The Mamluk sultans of Egypt wrote to both the king of Kanem and the king of Bornu (GHA IV ch.10, p.256). Lange asks whether the Bulala drive into Kanem was tied to the westward movement of Arab tribes, such as the Juhayna, after Christian Nubia broke up in the early 14th century (p.258). In about 1574, Idris Alawoma sent an embassy to Istanbul to secure Bornu's pilgrims and traders in Ottoman lands (GHA V ch.17, p.503). In the 17th century Wadai was founded with Bornu's blessing and stayed in its orbit (pp.511–512). In 1846, Wadai invaded Bornu at the mai's invitation (GHA VI ch.22, p.594).

Culture

Most of the Kanem-Bornu people had been Muslim since the 13th century (GHA IV ch.10, p.250). Mai 'Ali Gaji was invested as Khalifa about ten years before Askiya Muhammad of Songhay (GHA V ch.17, p.495). By the 17th century Bornu was the dominant power in the Bilad al-Sudan and a centre of learning (p.512). Kanuri words for maize ('Egyptian') and pawpaw ('melon from Egypt') suggest these crops came via Egypt on the pilgrim route (p.512).

Economy

Bornu sent slaves, eunuchs, skins, ivory, perfumes, leather goods and gold across the Sahara to North Africa and Egypt, in exchange for horses, armour, copper and bronze (GHA V ch.17, p.499). It controlled the salt sources at Muniyo, Bilma and Lake Chad (p.512) until Tuareg raids cost it Bilma around 1759 (p.512). Around 1400, Bir b. Idris complained to Egypt that Arabs were enslaving his Muslim subjects (GHA IV ch.10, p.250).
