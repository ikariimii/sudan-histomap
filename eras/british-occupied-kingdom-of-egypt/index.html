Geopolitics

For Sudan, this era is the Condominium. In January 1899 an Anglo-Egyptian agreement 'restored Egyptian rule in Sudan but as part of a condominium', designating territory south of the 22nd parallel as the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan; in practice the British governor-general ran it 'as if it were a colonial administration' (LOC1991_CONDO). After Sir Lee Stack was assassinated in Cairo in Nov 1924, Britain ordered all Egyptian troops and officials out of Sudan (LOC1991_CONDO). The 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty left Sudan's status unresolved, and Sudanese nationalists split over union with Egypt versus independence (LOC1991_NATIONALISM). In Feb 1953 London and Cairo signed an accord granting Sudan a three-year transition to self-government after Naguib accepted Sudanese self-determination (LOC1991_INDEP). The 1902 administrative line that later underlies the Halaib dispute also dates from this era (IBS18_1962) [CONTESTED]. On the Nile, the 1959 treaty notes that a 1929 Nile Waters Agreement 'only regulated a partial use of the natural river' (UNTS6519_1959) [1929 terms not read directly – VERIFY].

Culture

Seen from Sudan, Egypt's cultural weight in this era came from Cairo's Arabic literary and press renaissance: academies, a national library (1870), secular universities, a reformed al-Azhar and a modern school system produced a reading public, and the number of papers and journals grew from 169 in 1898 to 282 in 1913 (GHA VII ch.21, pp.555–556). Politically, Egyptian nationalism under Sa'd Zaghlul and the Wafd carried 'a romanticized notion of Egyptian sovereignty over the Sudan'; pro-Egyptian feeling in Sudan was often also anti-British and fed the shared myth of 'the Unity of the Nile Valley' (GHA VII ch.23, p.601). Economy

For Sudan, the Condominium economy rested partly on Egyptian money. Because taxes in Sudan were light, Britain and Egypt agreed that Egypt would give Sudan financial aid and loans for development and the budget: Egypt's contribution to the Sudanese budget was about £E 2.8 million for 1899–1916, and Egyptian loans about £E 5.4 million for 1900–10 (GHA VII ch.17, pp.455–456). The Gezira cotton scheme, started in 1911 by the Sudan government and the private Sudan Plantations Syndicate to supply Britain's textile industry, made cotton 'the mainstay of the country's economy' (LOC1991_CONDO; GHA VII ch.17, p.455, which gives the 40/35/25 tenant/government/syndicate split and the Sennar Dam of 1925).
