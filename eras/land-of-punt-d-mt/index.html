Geopolitics

Punt is almost agreed to lie in the Horn, but its boundaries are uncertain. The Wadi Gasus inscriptions confirm that Egyptians reached it by sea; Herzog's theory of an overland route through the Sudan is contested (GHA II ch.4, pp.144–145). Later, kings Rbh and Lmm took the title 'mukarrib of D'iamat and Saba'', a hereditary monarchy over clans in the northern Ethiopian highlands (ch.13, pp.353–354).

Culture

The Hatshepsut reliefs show Punt's people living in cone-shaped huts on piles (GHA II ch.4, p.147). In D'mt, a South Arabian period (5th–4th c. BCE) is marked by the Yeha temple, which has links to Marib (ch.13, pp.343, 345), and by the Sabaean gods Almaqah and 'Astar (p.352). De Contenson calls the result an 'Ethiopo-Sabaean' synthesis with 'Nilotic additions' (pp.354–355).

Economy

Egyptians sailed to Punt for incense that South Arabia had earlier supplied (GHA II ch.4, p.145). Hatshepsut's expedition brought back ivory, ebony, panther skins, myrrh trees, cattle, baboons and a giraffe (p.147). In D'mt, new farming methods and stone villages appear (ch.13, p.354); iron arrives from the 3rd c. BCE, and rings worn 'in the Meroitic fashion' may have served as money (p.357).
