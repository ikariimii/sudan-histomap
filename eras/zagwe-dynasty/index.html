Geopolitics

In the late 10th century, when a queen from the south sacked the Aksumite lands, the Ethiopian king appealed to the Coptic patriarch through the Nubian king George (Djirdjis) for a new metropolitan (GHA III ch.19, pp.565–566). The Zagwe dynasty then replaced the old line and moved the centre south while keeping many Aksumite traditions; it peaked in the 12th–13th centuries under kings such as Lalibela (p.568). A Zagwe expedition against Damot failed (GHA IV ch.17, p.431). In 1270, Yikunno-Amlak, backed by Shoan Christians and Muslims, overthrew the Zagwe (pp.430, 433).

Culture

The See of Aksum had been held since the 4th century by a Coptic bishop from Alexandria, and Ethiopia used the Egyptian liturgy (GHA III ch.19, p.565). Ge'ez was the language of court and church (p.568), and the later churches of Lalibela bear wall paintings (p.573). The Zagwe homeland of Wag and Lasta was Agew-speaking (GHA IV ch.17, p.425).

Economy

By the mid-13th century the Zagwe kingdom no longer used only the Dahlak sultanate as its Red Sea outlet and had begun to use the Zeila route through its southern provinces (GHA IV ch.17, p.430). Muslims controlled the caravan trade from the Gulf of Aden, and Christians in Shoa and Amhara acted as middlemen to the Zagwe heartland (p.432). The source gives no Zagwe trade with the Sudan.
