Geopolitics

Lange calls Kanem the first and largest state between the Nile and the Niger (GHA III ch.15, p.447). Al-Khuwarizmi (c. 840) mapped the Zaghawa south of the Fezzan and south of Nubian 'Alwa (p.447). In the 13th century the Zaghawa, pushed out of power in Kanem, pressed on the Daju of Darfur at the south end of the Darfur–Egypt route (p.440). Kanem then reached its height: in al-'Umari's day its empire ran from Zella 'on the Egyptian side' to Kaka (GHA IV ch.10, p.252). Whether it reached Darfur is not certain (p.252).

Culture

Islam reached Kanem through Kawar, probably first in the Ibadi form carried by Berber traders (GHA III ch.15, pp.451, 459). King Hummay (1075–86) was not the first Muslim ruler; Islam had been spreading for two centuries before him (p.459). The Saharan-language peoples of Kanem, Zaghawa, Teda-Daza and Kanuri, are Nilo-Saharan speakers (p.444). The Zaghawa are still recognized as a distinct people by the Arabs of Chad and the Sudan (p.440).

Economy

The camel, probably introduced from the Nile valley, opened Saharan crossings (GHA III ch.15, p.437). Lange conjectures that early trade with the Nile valley through Darfur and Kordofan mattered more than the Fezzan route (p.437). The king probably held a monopoly on acquiring slaves (p.459). Kawar exported alum as far as Egypt, and the Fezzan controlled both the north–south and the Egypt–west African routes (GHA IV ch.10, p.249).
